# Tower 1 as an animated film and a playable adventure

[View the ten-panel concept storyboard](concepts/tower-1-storyboard.png). Its panels summarize the major beats; the treatment below specifies thirteen scenes.

**Implementation update:** The full Tower now stages these principal beats in 3D, with a rewritten serious introduction and shorter scene timings. See [the current implementation](TOWER-1-3D.md). The detailed treatment below records the earlier proposed cut.

**Confirmed format:** cinematic story scenes plus playable arcade action. The game does not become an interactive movie made mainly of choices. The story is visibly acted, while movement, exploration, combat and mathematical work remain playable.

This is a proposed film treatment of the completed first-Tower script. It is a written treatment and storyboard, not a finished animated film. New lines below are suggested adaptations where needed to connect shots. The protagonist's identity and voice casting remain open. The Guidebot seen early is not Q.E.D.; Q.E.D.'s introduction remains later in the campaign.

## How the film and game share one world

Use the same environments and animated characters for cinematics and gameplay. A shot ends by moving the camera into the action view. The player inherits the character's position and intention. No return to a menu or mission spreadsheet.

During a formal math interaction, the camera settles at a real instrument. The threat clock pauses; ambient light and harmless idle animation may continue. The input remains large and clear. A correct calculation releases a visible mechanism. Assisted work asks for each intermediate result before revealing the next step. Story tension resumes after the learner completes or leaves the interaction, not while typing.

Cinematics are skippable and replayable, have subtitles, and remember completion across reloads. Skipping preserves the story state and objectives. Dialogue and music are separately adjustable. Essential cues have visual equivalents. Reduced-motion options remove violent shake and flashes without changing what happens.

## Complete story-beat treatment

Durations below are approximate cinematic screen time distributed across the level. They are not deadlines or estimates of the entire play session. The thirteen scenes total approximately **6 minutes 10 seconds**, surrounded by substantial playable exploration and action.

### 1. The arrival — 70 seconds

**Wide exterior:** Rain crosses a university campus after dark. Most buildings are asleep. Math Tower is lit from within. The camera tracks a service helicopter toward the roof.

**Inside the helicopter:** The protagonist watches the museum banner through the window. A small ordinary gesture—tightening a backpack strap—makes the character a person before the spectacle begins.

**Radio:** “Math Tower, this is Campus Security. We lost telemetry from your lower research levels. Do you copy?” Static. A faint voice: “…don't let it…”

**Exterior and reaction shots:** Lights go out downward through the Tower. The sound briefly collapses into a low pulse. The pilot fights the controls. A rotor wash drives rain sideways. The aircraft lands hard on the roof without graphic injury.

**Final shot and handoff:** The protagonist steps onto the roof. The pilot checks the damaged aircraft. The camera moves behind and beside the protagonist, framing the access door ahead. Control returns without changing scenes.

**Playable objective:** Reach the door and enter the first typed answer. A correct answer starts a small physical sequence: relays click, the latch withdraws, warm emergency light spills onto wet concrete, and the door opens.

### 2. The nearest safe reality — 15 seconds

**Floor 17, low medium shot:** A Guidebot lies on its side. It attempts a practiced greeting while one wheel turns uselessly.

“Welcome to Math Tower. Please proceed to the nearest…” Its eyes briefly lose focus. “…nearest safe reality.”

The protagonist leans closer. The machine shuts down. The camera holds the character's reaction, then gently returns control. The optional supplies remain a real explorable room.

### 3. The extra number — 8 seconds

**Floor 16, close-up:** The lift arrives before anyone calls it. The display reads 16, flashes 19, returns to 16.

**Reverse shot:** The protagonist looks into the empty car, then at the floor plaque. A small hesitation communicates the contradiction without an explanatory paragraph. The player decides how to proceed.

### 4. Please do not touch the exhibits — 18 seconds

**Floor 14, broad action composition:** Three Skitters feed on a cable beneath a suspended geometric sculpture. A Guidebot approaches: “Please do not touch the exhibits.”

One creature springs onto it. The robot spins and falls; the sculpture swings. The creatures turn toward the protagonist.

**Handoff:** The camera locks into a clear action angle as a creature visibly prepares its first leap. The player can dodge, stun, run, or release the hanging sculpture to obstruct pursuit. The consequential action is played, not completed by the cinematic.

### 5. Partial power — 18 seconds

After the player solves the Floor 12 panel, the protagonist operates a physical switch. Lights return in sections. Research Lift B shudders awake. Behind glass, another robot's eyes light up too.

A speaker crackles: “Is anyone there? If you're above twelve, do not come down.” A crash. “Actually… forget that. Get out.”

The protagonist looks upward toward the unreachable roof, then down the available route. Control returns with the changed machinery visible.

### 6. Larger inside — 28 seconds

**Floor 6, restrained tracking shot:** A normal laboratory door opens. At first the room appears ordinary. As the protagonist walks one step forward, the camera continues—and reveals a space far larger than the exterior building could contain.

A geometric projection passes through a wall without breaking it. An instrument measures the room twice and produces incompatible results.

An archived voice accompanies a briefly animated view of the researchers' earlier test: “The visualization isn't the problem. The coordinates are. The room is measuring larger inside than outside.” Another voice: “That's impossible.” “Yes.”

The camera returns to the protagonist at the threshold. The player explores and restores the emergency exit.

### 7. The Watcher — 10 seconds

**Floor 5:** The flashlight crosses glass. A tall still figure stands in the adjacent laboratory. Lightning reveals its outline. The protagonist turns directly toward it. It is gone.

One short controlled shot and a sound cue establish the presence. No monster biography or dialogue box explains it.

### 8. We opened something — 35 seconds

**Floor 3:** A frightened researcher is sitting against a wall. The scene is a close two-shot rather than a distant figure beside a text panel.

Researcher: “You're from upstairs?”

Protagonist: “What happened?”

Researcher: “We opened something.”

Protagonist: “What?”

A short, exhausted laugh. “We don't know.”

The researcher gestures toward the lobby route and explains that the exit override is in B3. Their gaze returns to a corridor: they are waiting for someone. The player may invite them to come or leave them waiting. Each choice has a short acted response; the scene does not declare a moral grade.

### 9. Something on the roof — 20 seconds

**Research lift between Floors 2 and 1:** Close framing makes the car feel confined. The light dies. A heavy impact above the protagonist. Dust falls. The ceiling dents.

The protagonist looks at the roof, then at the restart panel. The camera settles on the instrument and hands control back. The learner has unlimited calm time to calculate. Wrong answers do not trigger another attack or an escape that bypasses the learning.

After success, the motor catches. A shape passes overhead and disappears. The car reaches the lobby.

### 10. The other lobby — 15 seconds

**Floor 1:** Outside freedom is visible through glass. The protagonist reaches the doors; the emergency seal holds. The physical indicator points to the basement override.

Then the lobby changes: different furniture, different light, a statue that was never there. It returns before the protagonist can move.

If the researcher is present: “Did you see that?” “Yes.” “Good. I thought I was losing my mind.” Without the researcher, the same reveal holds through the protagonist's reaction and a quiet audio cue.

### 11. Again — 35 seconds

**B2:** The player activates a recording. The camera moves into the recorded scene: people, machines and actions, not a paragraph of archive text.

Voss: “Again.”

Researcher: “Mara, we've run it six times.”

Voss: “Then run it seven.”

Researcher: “Vale says the boundary conditions are unstable.”

Voss: “Vale says everything is unstable.”

While they speak, the apparatus moves into alignment and a correspondence diagram links differently shaped Towers. The recording ends before activation. Cut back to the same equipment, now damaged and silent. The names are introduced without an encyclopedic explanation.

### 12. The second Tower — 45 seconds

**B3:** Begin intimate: the protagonist's shoes stop at the edge of a circular platform. The camera rises, revealing an apparatus far larger than the preceding machinery.

Inside the ring is a room in another Tower, upside down. A figure moves within it. The camera cuts to the protagonist's face, then back to the impossible room.

An alarm: “Containment failure.” Control returns for the final typed override.

On success, the campus seals release—but the ring powers up. One Skitter drops through. Then three. Then a moving mass. The protagonist turns toward the escape route; the camera settles into action while the first hazard is still avoidable.

**Playable climax:** The B3→B2→B1→lobby escape uses earlier choices: freight power, supplies and the rescued researcher can change the experience. The Watcher walks behind the swarm, and the creatures avoid it. The player performs the escape.

### 13. Eighteen. Nineteen. — 53 seconds

**Lobby and exterior:** The doors open. The protagonist reaches the wet campus and turns to look back. Use a brief wide shot to let the audience breathe after the action.

Math Tower changes height: eighteen floors, nineteen, twenty-two, then eighteen. Its reflection in a puddle lags behind the building. The researcher, if rescued, sees it too.

A sound from inside the museum. The protagonist retreats toward the service route. In a final close-up, a maintenance sign points into darkness. A distant elevator bell rings once.

End the first Tower here. This is a cinematic promise of the next adventure; Campus Sprint and subsequent Towers are not implemented as part of this study or the first-Tower-only scope.

## What “fully presented as a movie” requires in production

- A shot list and storyboard for every mandatory story beat, including alternate versions for relevant player choices.
- Animated character poses, facial or equivalent expressive performance, interactions with props, and environmental animation. A still image with camera zoom is only an animatic aid, not the finished film.
- Voice performances or an approved voice-production plan, subtitles, music and deliberate sound design. No claim of completed voice work is made in this package.
- Shared scene assets and camera continuity so cinematics feel like the same world the player controls.
- A scene director that can pause, skip, replay and save its state independently of learning progress.
- Playable action between scenes: expressive movement, environmental interaction, anticipation and recovery, varied room geometry and meaningful decisions.
- A calm, accessible mathematical interaction that preserves cinematic context without taxing reaction speed or fine motor ability while the learner thinks.

The next production proof should show one complete film-to-play-to-math-to-world-consequence sequence in the chosen art direction. That sequence is the standard for the rebuilt game.
